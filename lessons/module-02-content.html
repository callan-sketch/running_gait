Running Gait Analysis Methodology

~25 min read Running Gait Analysis for Injury Prevention & Treatment

2.1 The Assessment Session

Module 1 established that unstructured visual gait observation is only moderately reliable, even among experienced clinicians, and that a standardised protocol closes much of that gap. This module sets out that protocol in full: how to set up the session, what to film, and — critically — what to actually look at once you have the footage.

A running gait analysis session has three parts, in this order: a brief subjective screen before the runner ever steps on a treadmill, the video capture itself, and then structured review of the footage. Skipping straight to filming without the screening step risks missing red flags (discussed in 2.7) that should change how — or whether — you proceed.

2.2 Camera Setup & Recording

The framework taught here follows the structure popularised by Souza's widely cited evidence-based videotaped running biomechanics analysis: a systematic, video-based protocol using two camera views and readily available 2D tools, built specifically so that simple 2D surrogates can stand in for the 3D variables the research literature actually studied.

  1. Two views, minimum. A lateral (sagittal plane) view and a posterior (frontal/transverse plane) view. A single camera angle cannot capture both stride mechanics and frontal-plane control.
  2. Consistent camera height and distance. Mount at roughly hip height, far enough back that several full gait cycles are visible in frame without the runner leaving the shot.
  3. Standardise the running surface and speed. A treadmill at a controlled, self-selected easy-run pace is the most practical clinical setup; note the exact speed so a later re-assessment is genuinely comparable.
  4. Allow a settling period. Give the runner 1–2 minutes to reach a natural rhythm before recording — treadmill gait in the first 30–60 seconds is rarely representative.
  5. Record enough cycles. Capture at least 10–15 seconds of continuous running per view so you can review several consecutive strides, not just one.
  6. Keep footwear and clothing consistent between sessions if you plan to compare pre- and post-intervention footage — fitted clothing at the hip and ankle make landmarks easier to see.

Standardised 2D video protocols like this one have shown meaningful test-retest reliability in follow-up validation work, which is exactly the point: the structure of the protocol is what buys you the reliability that free-form observation lacks.

Sources: Souza RB. An Evidence-Based Videotaped Running Biomechanics Analysis. Phys Med Rehabil Clin N Am. 2016;27(1):217–236. Dingenen B, Barton C, Janssen T, Benoit A, Malliaras P. Test-retest reliability of two-dimensional video analysis during running. Phys Ther Sport. 2018;33:40–47.

2.3 Sagittal Plane: What to Look For

From the lateral view, work through the same handful of variables on every runner, in the same order, every time. Consistency in what you screen for is as important to reliability as the camera setup itself.

Variable What to Look For
Foot strike pattern Where the foot first contacts the ground relative to the ankle — rearfoot, midfoot, or forefoot.
Overstriding Hip flexion with the knee still extending just before initial contact, placing the foot well ahead of the body's centre of mass.
Trunk lean Excessive forward or backward lean relative to a roughly neutral upright posture through stance.
Vertical oscillation How much the centre of mass (roughly, the top of the head) bounces vertically stride to stride — visually, whether the runner looks like they're "bouncing" excessively.
Knee flexion at initial contact A very straight, extended knee at contact is one of the sagittal-plane hallmarks that accompanies overstriding.

2.4 Posterior Plane: What to Look For

The posterior view is where most of the frontal-plane compensations relevant to injury show up — and where reliability varies the most between variables, so this is exactly where a structured method earns its keep (see 2.6).

Variable What to Look For
Pelvic drop (contralateral) The unsupported side of the pelvis dropping below level during single-leg stance on the other side.
Hip adduction / crossover The stance-leg thigh angling in toward, or past, the body's midline — visible as the foot landing close to or across an imaginary centreline.
Knee position (valgus / "knee window") Whether a visible gap remains between the knees during stance, or whether the knee collapses inward.
Heel whip The heel kicking excessively inward or outward during the swing phase, just after toe-off.
Rearfoot eversion The degree and, notably, the duration of inward rolling of the heel through stance — both matter (see 2.6 and Module 3).

2.5 Measuring Cadence

Cadence (steps per minute) is the one variable in this entire framework with genuinely strong, repeated intervention evidence behind it, so it deserves its own measurement step rather than being estimated by eye.

  • Manual count method: count footfalls on one side for 30 seconds, multiply by 4 for total steps per minute — quick, requires no equipment, and accurate enough for clinical purposes.
  • GPS running watch or phone app: most modern running watches display real-time cadence, which is useful both for baseline measurement and later for retraining feedback in Module 5.
  • Typical recreational cadence commonly falls in the 150–170 steps-per-minute range at an easy pace, though this varies with height, speed, and individual stride mechanics — there is no single "correct" number, only a runner's own baseline and the modifiable 5–10% increase discussed in Modules 3 and 5.

2.6 Why the Protocol Matters

Not all of the variables above are equally reliable to score by eye — which is exactly why the standardised method matters more than any individual clinician's experience. One reliability study asked raters to independently score several categorical gait variables from video, twice each, and compared agreement using Cohen's kappa (values above roughly 0.75 are generally considered excellent agreement, 0.40–0.75 fair-to-good, and below 0.40 poor):

Variable Inter-Rater Agreement
Overstriding Excellent (κ up to 0.92)
Knee window Excellent (κ up to 0.85)
Heel whip Excellent (κ up to 0.85)
Foot progression angle Good-to-excellent (κ 0.65–0.85)
Hindfoot eversion Poor-to-fair, highly variable between raters (κ as low as 0.24)
Pelvic drop Poor, the least reliable of the variables studied (κ as low as 0.05)
Clinical note

Overstriding, knee window, heel whip, and foot progression angle can be scored with reasonable confidence from video alone. Pelvic drop and hindfoot eversion cannot — clinicians should treat findings on these two variables as provisional, and where possible, corroborate them against other assessment findings (strength testing, palpation, symptom pattern) rather than acting on the video alone.

Reference: Inter-rater reliability data from a three-rater 2D kinematic video analysis study assessing nine categorical running gait variables (2015).

2.7 Building the Screening Intake

Before filming, a brief structured intake accomplishes two things: it gives context to what you're about to see on video, and it screens for anything that should change your plan before you put a runner on a treadmill at all.

Training History

  • Current weekly volume, and how it has changed over the past 4–6 weeks — a sudden increase is one of the most consistently cited risk factors in the running-injury literature, independent of any gait finding.
  • Recent changes in surface, terrain, or footwear.
  • Goal event or target, and timeframe — this shapes how aggressively you can modify anything.

Injury History

  • Current symptoms: location, onset, behaviour with activity and rest, 0–10 severity.
  • Prior running-related injuries and how they resolved.
Red flag screening

Before proceeding with a routine gait analysis, screen for features suggestive of a bone stress injury: focal bony tenderness, pain that progresses to interfere with walking or is present at rest or at night, and pain that doesn't settle with brief rest. These warrant a more cautious work-up (and likely imaging referral) before any running-based assessment or loading intervention — this is exactly the population Module 4's treatment-sequencing guidance asks you to identify early.

With the screening complete and footage captured using this protocol, you have a standardised, defensible record to interpret — which is exactly what Module 3 walks through next: turning these specific observations into a clinical hypothesis about what's driving a runner's symptoms.

Summary

A structured, two-view video protocol — consistent camera setup, a defined list of sagittal and posterior-plane variables, and an objective cadence measurement — closes most of the reliability gap identified in Module 1. Not every variable is equally trustworthy from video alone: overstriding, knee window, heel whip, and foot progression angle can be scored with confidence, while pelvic drop and hindfoot eversion need corroboration. Pairing this with a brief, red-flag-aware screening intake completes the assessment session and sets up Module 3's interpretation work.
