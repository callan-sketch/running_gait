Injury-Specific Treatment Pathways

~32 min read Running Gait Analysis for Injury Prevention & Treatment

4.1 From Hypothesis to Treatment Plan

Module 3 gave you a set of gait-based hypotheses for each of the five dominant running injuries. This module takes each pathology and builds the treatment pathway that follows once a working diagnosis is confirmed on history, palpation, and special testing — not gait findings alone.

A consistent thread runs through all five conditions below: strengthening and progressive loading of the relevant muscle groups have the strongest and most consistent evidence base of any intervention category. Gait retraining is addressed separately in 4.7 and built out fully in Module 5 — it is a targeted tool for specific presentations, not a default first-line intervention for every runner who walks through the door.

4.2 Patellofemoral Pain

Given the hip-adduction mechanism identified in Module 3, it follows that hip- and knee-targeted strengthening is the best-supported intervention for PFP, and the trial evidence bears this out directly.

  • Combined hip and knee strengthening — a randomised trial comparing a combined hip-and-knee strengthening protocol against knee-strengthening alone found the combined group achieved significantly better pain and function outcomes, supporting a hip-inclusive approach over a knee-only quadriceps programme.
  • Hip abductor and external rotator strengthening — targets the same hip adduction pattern flagged as the strongest prospective predictor in Module 3; typically progressed from open-chain (side-lying, banded) through to closed-chain, functional loading.
  • Quadriceps strengthening — remains a core component; PFP has long been associated with quadriceps weakness independent of the hip mechanism, and closed-chain strengthening is generally preferred to isolated open-chain knee extension for symptom tolerance.
  • Load management — reducing aggravating volume or intensity in the short term while strength is built, rather than complete rest, which does not address the underlying capacity deficit.

Source: Nascimento LR, Teixeira-Salmela LF, Souza RB, Resende RA. Hip and Knee Strengthening Is More Effective Than Knee Strengthening Alone for Reducing Pain and Improving Activity in Individuals With Patellofemoral Pain: A Systematic Review With Meta-analysis. J Orthop Sports Phys Ther. 2018;48(1):19–31.

4.3 Iliotibial Band Syndrome

ITBS treatment follows directly from the reduced hip abductor strength identified consistently in the Module 3 literature, and a systematic review of hip-strengthening interventions specifically for ITBS supports the approach.

Intervention Evidence & Notes
Hip abductor strengthening A systematic review found hip abductor strengthening programmes were associated with reductions in ITBS pain reported across included studies ranging from roughly 27% to 100%, alongside improved hip abductor strength.
Gluteus medius & posterior chain strengthening Typically progressed from isolated abduction work to functional single-leg loading (step-downs, single-leg bridges) that better replicates the demands of stance phase.
Short-term load reduction Reducing volume, hill running, or cambered-surface running while symptomatic and strength is being rebuilt.
Foam rolling / soft tissue work Commonly used adjunctively for symptom modulation; evidence supports short-term symptomatic relief rather than addressing the underlying strength deficit, so it should not replace strengthening.

Source: Ferber R, Kendall KD, Pohl MB. Runners with iliotibial band syndrome demonstrate atypical hip and knee kinematics. J Orthop Sports Phys Ther. 2010;40(2):52–58; Fredericson M, Wolf C. Iliotibial band syndrome in runners: innovations in treatment. Sports Med. 2005;35(5):451–459.

4.4 Medial Tibial Stress Syndrome

Before treating

Re-confirm the red-flag screening from Module 2 (2.7) before beginning a loading-based programme. Focal bony tenderness, night pain, or pain at rest should prompt referral for imaging to rule out tibial bone stress injury before proceeding with the approach below.

Once bone stress injury has been screened out, MTSS management centres on progressive, graded loading of the lower leg musculature rather than passive modalities alone.

  • Progressive calf and tibialis posterior loading — a structured lower-leg exercise programme, progressed from isometric through to plyometric loading over weeks, has been shown to improve outcomes compared to standard care in runners with MTSS.
  • Ankle dorsiflexion mobility work — targets the reduced static dorsiflexion range identified as a consistent finding in Module 3; a stiff ankle may drive the compensatory eversion pattern implicated in this condition.
  • Graded return-to-run volume — MTSS is fundamentally a training-load condition, so any structural intervention needs to sit alongside deliberate volume and intensity management, covered fully in Module 5.

Source: Moen MH, Holtslag L, Bakker E, et al. The treatment of medial tibial stress syndrome in athletes; a randomized clinical trial. Sports Med Arthrosc Rehabil Ther Technol. 2012;4:12; Becker J, James S, Wayner R, Osternig L, Chou LS. Am J Sports Med. 2017;45(11):2614–2621.

4.5 Achilles Tendinopathy

Loading is the cornerstone of evidence-based Achilles tendinopathy management. The two best-studied protocols are eccentric-only loading and heavy slow resistance (HSR), and head-to-head trial evidence has clarified how they compare.

Protocol Evidence & Notes
Eccentric loading (Alfredson protocol) The original and most widely studied protocol; strong evidence for mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy specifically, less consistent results for insertional presentations.
Heavy slow resistance (HSR) A randomised trial comparing HSR directly against eccentric training found both produced comparable improvements in pain and function at 12-month follow-up, with runners reporting higher satisfaction and better adherence to the HSR protocol.
Isometric loading Commonly used in the acute, highly irritable stage for short-term analgesic effect before progressing into one of the isotonic protocols above.

Given the Module 3 finding on forefoot strike increasing Achilles load, a runner who has self-adopted a forefoot pattern to manage a different (e.g. patellofemoral) complaint and now presents with Achilles symptoms is a specific scenario where re-examining that gait choice — not just prescribing more loading — belongs in the plan.

Source: Beyer R, Kongsgaard M, Hougs Kjær B, et al. Heavy Slow Resistance Versus Eccentric Training as Treatment for Achilles Tendinopathy: A Randomized Controlled Trial. Am J Sports Med. 2015;43(7):1704–1711.

4.6 Plantar Fasciitis

Plantar fasciitis treatment has shifted over the past two decades from stretching-only protocols toward an approach that combines fascia-specific stretching with progressive strength training.

  • Plantar-fascia-specific stretching — a randomised trial found plantar-fascia-specific stretching produced significantly better outcomes than standard Achilles-stretching protocols at 8 weeks, and the benefit was maintained at long-term follow-up.
  • High-load strength training — more recent trial evidence supports progressive strength training of the plantar flexors (calf raises, progressing to loaded single-leg variants) as comparable or superior to stretching alone for medium-term outcomes, with the added benefit of addressing broader lower-limb loading capacity.
  • Footwear and load review — a practical check on cushioning, wear, and recent changes in training surface or volume, alongside the strength-and-stretch programme rather than as a substitute for it.

Source: DiGiovanni BF, Nawoczenski DA, Lintal ME, et al. Tissue-specific plantar fascia-stretching exercise enhances outcomes in patients with chronic heel pain. J Bone Joint Surg Am. 2003;85(7):1270–1277; Rathleff MS, Mølgaard CM, Fredberg U, et al. High-load strength training improves outcome in patients with plantar fasciitis: A randomized controlled trial. Scand J Med Sci Sports. 2015;25(3):e292–e300.

4.7 When Gait Retraining Belongs in the Plan

Every condition above is treated first and foremost with progressive loading and strengthening — that is where the strongest evidence sits, and it is where most treatment plans should start. Gait retraining is not a replacement for that foundation; it is an additional tool for a specific subset of runners.

Clinical reasoning

Consider gait retraining as an adjunct when a runner has a clearly identified, modifiable gait pattern from Module 2's assessment (most commonly overstriding / low cadence, given the strength of the cadence evidence covered in Module 1), symptoms that have plateaued despite an adequate strengthening programme, or a pattern directly implicated in their specific injury from the Module 3 mapping.

Module 5 builds out exactly how to run a gait-retraining intervention — cueing strategies, dosage, and how to integrate it with the load-management and return-to-run planning that ties this whole course together.

Summary

Progressive, condition-specific strengthening is the common thread across all five injuries: combined hip-and-knee work for PFP, hip abductor strengthening for ITBS, graded lower-leg loading for MTSS (after red-flag screening), eccentric or heavy-slow-resistance loading for Achilles tendinopathy, and fascia-specific stretching paired with strength training for plantar fasciitis. Gait retraining sits alongside this foundation as a targeted adjunct for runners with a clearly identified, modifiable pattern — not a first-line substitute for loading. Module 5 now turns to how gait retraining and structured return-to-run programming are actually delivered.
